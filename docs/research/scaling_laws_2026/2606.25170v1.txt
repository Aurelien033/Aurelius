                                           1   Introduction

                                           Exogenous i.i.d. contexts arise naturally when independent external conditions are revealed before
                                           the agent acts. A canonical example is Tetris-like games ([Algorta and Şimşek, 2019, Bodoia and
                                           Puranik, 2012, Gabillon et al., 2013]), where the board configuration is the controlled state, the
                                           incoming piece is the exogenous context, the action is its placement and rotation, and the reward is
                                           the score or number of cleared lines. The i.i.d. assumption corresponds to versions where pieces are
                                           independently sampled from a fixed distribution. The same structure appears in online advertising
                                           (Badanidiyuru et al. [2021], flajolet and Jaillet [2017], Abhishek et al. [2020]), the controlled state is
                                           the advertiser’s budget, and the exogenous context is the arriving user or query profile. The action
                                           is a bid and the reward is a conversion or click value. In ride-hailing ([Qin et al., 2020, Bao et al.,
                                           2025]), the controlled state es the fleet configuration, whereas the context is the incoming ride request,
                                           including origin, destination or timing. The action assigns drivers, and the reward captures revenue
                                           or waiting-time penalties. In e-commerce pricing ([Ban and Keskin, 2021, Wang et al., 2025, Chen
                                           et al., 2022, Zhao et al., 2026]), the controlled state includes inventory or platform dynamics, while
                                           the context is the customer or session information observed before pricing. The action is the posted
                                           price and the reward is the resulting revenue or profit.
1.1   Related work.

The sample complexity of tabular reinforcement learning with a generative model is well understood.
Azar et al. [2012] proved minimax PAC bounds for discounted MDPs, with optimal dependence
Θ(|X
e     ||A|/((1 − γ)3 ε2 )). Near-optimal algorithms were then obtained through variance-reduced
q-value iteration [Sidford et al., 2019] and model-based plug-in methods [Agarwal et al., 2020]. For
model-free methods, variance-reduced Q-learning is minimax optimal up to logs [Wainwright, 2019],
whereas standard Q-learning is generally suboptimal, with tight (1 − γ)−4 dependence when |A| ≥ 2
[Li et al., 2023]. Instance-dependent guarantees for policy evaluation and best-policy identification
were also studied in generative and online settings [Pananjady and Wainwright, 2020, Marjani et al.,
2021]. Our work departs from this line by replacing the state-action generative model with samples
from an exogenous context distribution.
A complementary line exploits structural knowledge of the dynamics to improve sample efficiency.
[Alharbi et al., 2024] study additive-disturbance MDPs with known transition function and i.i.d.
unknown disturbances, obtaining bounds independent of state and action cardinalities. [Modi
et al., 2019] consider model-based RL with a given simulator ensemble, where the true MDP is
approximated by state-dependent convex combinations of base transition and reward models; their
complexity depends on the number of mixing parameters rather than on |S| or |A|.
Several recent works study RL with exogenous components. In ExoMDPs, [Efroni et al., 2022]
consider action-independent, reward-irrelevant exogenous variables, so the goal is to learn a policy
that ignores them. [Trimponias and Dietterich, 2026] allow policy-relevant exogenous variables,
but with an additive action-independent reward component that can be subtracted without changing
optimal policies. [Sinclair et al., 2023] study resource-management Exo-MDPs where endogenous
dynamics and rewards are known deterministic functions of actions and exogenous inputs, leaving
uncertainty only over the input distribution. Finally, [Wan et al., 2025] obtain regret bounds for
Exo-MDPs via tabular and linear-mixture MDPs, with complexity driven by the exogenous state space
rather than by the full state-action space. Our setting differs: contexts are observed, decision-relevant,
and enter PAC estimation of context-dependent Bellman operators.
Our setting also resembles model predictive control [Camacho and Bordons, 2013], where known
dynamics are driven by exogenous disturbances, such as price, weather, or demand signals, akin to our
contexts. Stochastic and scenario-based MPC [Calafiore and Campi, 2006, Mesbah, 2016] treat these
disturbances through samples or forecasts and compute receding-horizon controls conditioned on their
realizations. However, this literature focuses mainly on stability, recursive feasibility, and constraint
satisfaction, rather than sample-complexity guarantees for learning from disturbance samples.
Our framework also directly subsumes reinforcement learning with one-step transition lookahead
(Pla et al. [2026], Merlis [2024]). In that setting, before acting, the agent observes the collection of
next states that would be reached under the available actions; this collection can be viewed as a single
exogenous context, sampled by the environment and revealed before the action. Lu et al. [2025]
study MDPs with multi-step transition predictions and provide finite-sample guarantees for learning
a prediction-aware Bayesian value function. In the perfect one-step transition-lookahead case, our
results sharpen this sample-complexity analysis.


1.2   Contribution.

finite state space X , action space A, and context space Z. The agent has access to a generative model

2.1   Exogenous contextual Markov decision process.

Model. We consider finite discounted Markov decision processes with exogenous i.i.d. contexts.
A model is a tuple M = (X , Z, A, P, r, µ, γ), where X is the finite controlled state space, Z is
the context space, A is the finite action space, P is a controlled transition kernel, r is a reward
function, µ is the context distribution, and γ ∈ [0, 1) is the discount factor. The transition kernel is
P : X × A × Z → ∆(X ), so that P (· | x, a, z) is the law of the next controlled state after taking
action a in controlled state x under context z. The reward function is r : X × A × Z → [0, 1]
Throughout the paper, for simplicity, r is deterministic and known. Interactions are as follows:
at each time t ≥ 0, the learner observes the augmented state (Xt , Zt ) ∈ X × Z, chooses an
action At ∈ A, receives reward r(Xt , At , Zt ), and the next state-context is generated according to
Xt+1 ∼ P (· | Xt , At , Zt ), Zt+1 ∼ µ(·), where Zt+1 is independent of the past.

is a map π : X × Z → A, under which the action at time t is At = π(Xt , Zt ). Restrict-
ing to policies that ignore Zt would be suboptimal and would not capture the decision prob-

2.2   Learning objectives

Our objective is to determine the sample complexity of policy evaluation (PE), best value estimation
(BVE), and best-policy extraction (BPE), which are the tasks of estimating V π (x, z), V ∗ (x, z), and
π ∗ (x, z), respectively. The learner has access to the generative oracle : depending on the regime, an
oracle call returns either a sample from the context distribution µ, or a transition sample generated
from P at a chosen state-context-action triplet. Oracle calls may be made before the query point
(x, z) is known, during an offline learning phase, or after (x, z) is revealed, when answering that
query.

3.3   One-step perfect transition lookahead

We now apply our results above by specialising them to a model of independent interest: the perfect
one-step transition lookahead ([Pla et al., 2026, Merlis, 2024]). The model is built on top of a finite
discounted tabular MDP M0 = (X , A, P0 , r, γ), with state space X , action space A, transition
kernel P0 : X × A → ∆(X ), reward r : X × A → [0, 1], and discount factor γ ∈ [0, 1).
In the look-ahead model, at each time step t, before committing to an action, the agent observes
the entire tensor of hypothetical successors that would be reached from every (x, a) pair under
P0 . Formally, the context space is Z := X X ×A , and a context z = (z(x, a))(x,a)∈X ×A ∈ Z
specifies, for every (x, a), the next state z(x, a) ∈ X that would be reached if action a were taken
at stateNx. Contexts are drawn independently across coordinates from the underlying kernel P0 :
µ =        (x,a)∈X ×A P0 (· | x, a). Having observed (Xt , Zt ), the agent picks At , receives reward
r(Xt , At ), and transitions deterministically to Xt+1 = Zt (Xt , At ), while a fresh independent context
Zt+1 ∼ µ is drawn for the next step.

5     Conclusion
We introduce a PAC learning framework for discounted MDPs with exogenous i.i.d. contexts, in
which sample complexity is decomposed into an offline learning budget nlearn and a cost-per-query
mquery . The central question we addressed is whether the statistical price of large context spaces is
unavoidable, and our results give a negative answer in two distinct regimes. When the transition kernel
and the reward are known and only the context distribution must be learned, we proved that PE, BVE,
and BPE all admit decision-time mechanism with complexity (nlearn , mquery ) = (O(β          e 3 /ε2 ), 0).
The rate is independent of |Z| and minimax optimal up to logarithmic factors. Specializing to
the perfect one-step transition lookahead, we obtained (O(β     e 3 /ε2 ), 0) lookahead samples, which
                      3            2
corresponds to e O(β |X ||A|/ε ) tabular transitions and tightens by a factor β the bound of Lu et al.
[2025]. When both µ and P are unknown, we focused on PE and showed that the sample complexity
remains independent of the cardinality of the context space. The algorithm learns an averaged value
V̄ π ∈ R|X | offline, and lifts it at query time by sampling the unknown
                                                                          transition kernel at the realized
                                                                                             
(x, z). This gives the tight complexity (nlearn , mquery ) = O      e |X |β 3 /ε2 , Oe β 2 /ε2 , with no
dependence on |Z|. Thus, unknown dynamics do not force the learner to estimate values on the
augmented space X × Z; they only make the decision-time Bellman lift stochastic. Several questions
remain open. A first direction is to understand the full offline/online tradeoff when both µ and P are
unknown. Our lower bound is matched, up to logarithmic factors, at two points: the purely online
rollout point nlearn = 0, and the query-efficient point obtained by our reusable evaluator. Whether
the same lower bound is tight for intermediate values of nlearn remains open. A second direction
is to extend the fully unknown analysis to BVE and BPE. The main difficulty is that the contextual
Bellman maximum must then be estimated from transition samples, which creates a selection bias
when several actions are nearly tied. An other direction is to relax the i.i.d. assumption on the
contexts and allow the exogenous process to be Markovian, or more generally mixing. In that case,
the averaged Bellman operator remains meaningful, but concentration and variance propagation must
account for temporal dependence. Finally, it would be interesting to move beyond tabular controlled
state spaces. For instance, in linear or otherwise structured MDPs, one may hope to learn an averaged
value representation whose complexity scales with the dimension of the controlled state space, rather
than with the size of the augmented space X × Z.
